Somewhere, Estonia

Western Battery

Coastal Defense Battery No. 15

Peter the Great’s Sea Fortress once crossed the Baltic Sea, stretching 36 kilometres across its narrowest point between Tallinn, Estonia, and Porkkala, Finland. Designed as a series of batteries, bunkers, and supply routes across both shores and many islands, it aimed to block sea access to the then Russian capital Saint Petersburg.

The decision to construct this naval fortress came after Russia's defeat by Japan in the 1905 Battle of Tsushima, which resulted in the loss of nearly the entire Baltic Fleet. This left the sea route to Saint Petersburg unprotected, until a new Baltic fleet could be built.

One trace of this protection line can be found on a small island on the Estonian coast. Two heavy batteries were built right next to each other, each with 12-inch/52-calibre naval guns with a firing range of around 26 kilometres. Or at least, two guns were the plan, but by 1917, while the east gun tower was fully completed, the west gun was never installed. The two gun towers were supported by a command post, a diesel power plant, and an underground corridor connecting the two towers.

Both naval gun towers were deliberately blown up on 27 February 1918 by retreating Russian Bolshevik forces. At that time during World War I, the German army was advancing into Estonia. Later in 1941, when Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union and again advanced into Estonia, history repeated itself, and the then repaired batteries were blown up for a second and final time.

Today, both towers still remain in ruins, partly rusting and partly overgrown, with the main attraction of both being the gigantic steel rings which once allowed the cannons to rotate.
