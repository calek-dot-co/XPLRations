Soviet Roadside Murals

Tskhvarichamia, Georgia

Leaving Tbilisi, the transition to rural life is instantaneous. As the road starts to wind through the hilly countryside towards the Sabaduri Forest, the view out the car window combines nature with a little bit of history. Right before a hairpin turn, you'll find over thirty Soviet-era tile murals lining the roadside.

The text reads ‘Pioneer Town Tsiskari’ in both Georgian Mkhedruli script (top two lines) and Russian Cyrillic script (bottom two lines).

As you continue up the road, the tile murals get more diverse. Each large horizontal mural is separated by smaller vertical murals, which transition themes between different parts of Soviet life. From sea exploration to space exploration, and everything in between.

The text reads ‘May There Always Be Sunshine’ in both Georgian (top line) and Russian (bottom line).

This is a quote from a popular Soviet children's anti-war song from 1962. The song sings of a clear sky and peace. You can listen to it in Russian with English subtitles here on YouTube.

Further up the hill, you'll find a change in what the mural panels depict. Gone are the big topics of ideals and exploration, and in come the more local depictions of Georgian history, craftsmanship, and science.

Remember where you came from.

This mural shows a Dedabodzi pillar, literally ‘mother pillar’. Holding up the roof of a traditional Georgian home, this thick and heavy wooden beam is often carved with traditional symbols. Flanking the pillar are the husband, wife, and many guardians wearing Chokhas.

Appreciate the hard work in creating a home.

This panel celebrates the iconic, ornate wooden balconies (called ‘shushabandi’ or oriel balconies) that are common in Tbilisi and other Georgian towns.

Look forward to the stars, science and the future.

The Sun, the eight planets, Sputnik, STEM education, and grapevines combine in this image to depict the wonders of modern science and space exploration.

And finally, as you near the end of the murals, they get weirder, catering more to the younger kids of the Pioneer Camps. Vegetable people, frogs, and jesters bring this chain of murals about Soviet and Georgian life to a fitting end, that is, if you were a child on the way to camp in the 1970s.
